Texas Law & the UDCA Role

Legal framework under Texas Health and Safety Code Chapter 168

Legal AuthorityThis course is required under Texas Health and Safety Code §168.005, which directs the Texas Diabetes Council to develop training guidelines for Unlicensed Diabetes Care Assistants (UDCAs).
What is a UDCA?

An Unlicensed Diabetes Care Assistant (UDCA) is a non-medical school staff member trained to assist students with diabetes. UDCAs are not nurses — they work under the supervision of the school principal and follow care plans written by licensed healthcare professionals.

Who Requires UDCAs?
HSC § 168.004 — Principal's Duty

School principals must seek and make efforts to designate staff as UDCAs when students with diabetes are enrolled in their school.

How many UDCAs?

Schools without a full-time nurse: At least 3 trained UDCAs required.
Schools with a full-time nurse: At least 1 trained UDCA required.

HSC § 168.004(b) — Supervision

UDCAs must be supervised by the school principal.

Who Trains UDCAs?

The school nurse assigned to the campus is responsible for training UDCAs. If no school nurse is assigned, a healthcare professional with expertise in diabetes care must provide training.

When Must Training Occur?

Per HSC §168.005(c), training must occur:

  • Before the beginning of the school year
  • As soon as possible after a student with diabetes enrolls at a campus that previously had none
  • As soon as possible after a student at a campus receives a new diabetes diagnosis
Key Documents Every UDCA Must Know
DocumentCreated ByPurpose
DMTP — Diabetes Management & Treatment PlanStudent's physicianPhysician orders to follow at school
DMMP — Diabetes Medical Management PlanStudent's diabetes care team + parentsDescribes complete diabetes care plan
IHP — Individual Health PlanSchool nurse + principalCampus-specific implementation of DMMP
Record Keeping RequiredThe school nurse or principal must maintain a copy of training guidelines and all records associated with training, per HSC §168.005(e).
Student Rights Under Chapter 168

Per HSC §168.008, students must be permitted to manage their own diabetes care anywhere on campus, including:

  • Checking and monitoring blood glucose levels
  • Administering diabetes medication per their DMTP
  • Managing low and high blood sugar as quickly as possible
  • Carrying necessary diabetes supplies and equipment (including a smartphone)
  • Performing self-care in the classroom or any location at school
ImportantSchools cannot require a student with diabetes to go to the nurse's office to check blood glucose, have a snack, or administer insulin — unless specified in the student's IHP with parental agreement.

Diabetes Basics

Understanding the disease, its types, and how it is managed

What Is Diabetes?

Diabetes is a chronic endocrine disorder involving insulin — either the pancreas no longer produces it, or the body cannot use it properly. Without insulin, glucose (sugar) remains in the bloodstream rather than entering cells for energy, leading to elevated blood glucose levels that can damage major organs and blood vessels over time.

The Three Main Types
TypeMechanismInsulin Required?School-Age Prevalence
Type 1Pancreas stops making insulin entirelyAlways — lifelongMost common in school-age children
Type 2Pancreas makes insufficient insulin or body resists itSometimes (or oral meds / diet)Increasing in adolescents
GestationalDiagnosed during pregnancy onlySometimesNot applicable in K-12
You CANNOT tell by lookingIt is not possible to look at a student and determine they have diabetes. The condition is diagnosed only by a blood test. Never make assumptions about a student's health status from their appearance.
The Role of the Pancreas

The pancreas produces insulin, which acts as a "key" that allows glucose to enter the body's cells. Without this key, cells starve for energy while glucose accumulates dangerously in the blood. For students with Type 1, this process has stopped completely — insulin must come from external sources every day.

Goals of Diabetes Management
  • Maintain blood glucose within the target range specified in the student's IHP
  • Prevent complications from persistently out-of-range blood glucose
  • Allow full participation in academic programs and extracurricular activities
  • Follow prescribed meal plans, physical activity schedules, and medication
  • See a healthcare provider routinely
Key PointStudents with diabetes are NOT limited from extracurricular activities — sports, band, debate, plays, orchestra. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act and the ADA prohibit discrimination against students with disabilities, including diabetes.
Blood Glucose Monitoring Frequency

There is no single universal schedule. A student's IHP will specify how often and when to check blood glucose while at school (e.g., before lunch, after physical activity). The UDCA follows those orders exactly — not a general rule of thumb.

Hypoglycemia & Hyperglycemia

Recognizing and responding to blood sugar emergencies

Hypoglycemia (Low Blood Sugar)

  • Hunger
  • Dizziness
  • Excessive sweating
  • Trembling / shakiness
  • Inability to concentrate
  • Confusion
  • Headache
  • Slurred speech
  • Seizure (severe)
  • Loss of consciousness (severe)

Hyperglycemia (High Blood Sugar)

  • Excessive thirst
  • Frequent urination
  • Drowsiness
  • Irritability
  • Blurred vision
  • Ketones in urine
  • Nausea
  • Fruity breath odor
Critical RulePhysical symptoms ALONE cannot confirm hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia. The ONLY definitive way to determine blood glucose status is to measure it with a glucose meter or CGM. Always check before treating, unless the student is unconscious.
Treating Mild to Moderate Hypoglycemia
  • Check the student's blood glucose with a meter to confirm low reading
  • Follow the student's IHP for the specific treatment order
  • Administer fast-acting glucose (glucose tablets, juice, regular soda) as ordered
  • Wait 15 minutes, then recheck blood glucose
  • If still low, repeat treatment per IHP
  • Document the episode, time, reading, treatment, and outcome
Treating Severe Hypoglycemia
Emergency Protocol — Severe Hypoglycemia If a student collapses or has a seizure:
1. Do NOT give anything by mouth if unconscious
2. Administer glucagon per the student's IHP
3. Call 911 immediately after administering glucagon
4. Call the parent/guardian
5. Place the student on their side
6. Stay with the student until EMS arrives
Treating Hyperglycemia
  • Check blood glucose to confirm elevated reading
  • Check urine or blood ketones if ordered in the IHP
  • Follow the student's IHP — this may include administering a correction dose of insulin
  • Encourage the student to drink water
  • Document all readings, treatments, and observations
  • Contact parent/guardian if readings remain out of range
Physical Activity and Blood Sugar

Exercise can lower blood glucose levels and increase insulin sensitivity. Before any physical activity, a student with diabetes should:

  • Monitor blood glucose before, during, and after scheduled physical activity
  • Adjust insulin doses according to the IHP if applicable
  • Have fast-acting glucose available to treat potential hypoglycemia
  • Have snacks available for moderate hypoglycemia episodes
Physical Activity TipThe most common complication during physical activity in students with diabetes is hypoglycemia. Always ensure the student has glucose sources available during exercise and recess.

Monitoring & Technology

Blood glucose meters, CGMs, ketone testing, and documentation

Blood Glucose Monitoring — Fingerstick Method
  • Wash hands with soap and water (or use antiseptic wipe)
  • Put on latex-free gloves
  • Insert test strip into the blood glucose meter
  • Use the lancet device to prick the side of the fingertip
  • Apply a small drop of blood to the test strip
  • Read and record the result, time, and any student observations
  • Dispose of lancet in the sharps container immediately
  • Compare reading to the IHP target range and follow orders
Continuous Glucose Monitor (CGM)

A CGM uses a sensor inserted under the skin that measures interstitial blood glucose levels at regular intervals throughout the day. CGMs transmit readings to a receiver or smartphone without requiring a fingerstick for every reading.

UDCA CGM Responsibilities Know how to read the CGM display and alarms. Know the backup plan if the CGM malfunctions. Know when calibration of equipment is needed. Always follow IHP orders for what actions to take based on CGM readings.
Ketone Testing

Ketones are chemicals the liver produces when breaking down fats — this occurs when cells can't get glucose due to insufficient insulin. High ketones signal possible diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a medical emergency. Ketone testing is typically ordered when blood glucose is elevated (often above 250 mg/dL).

MethodSampleWhen Used
Urine ketone stripsUrine sampleMost common in schools
Blood ketone meterFingerstick bloodMore accurate, increasingly common

Record ketone results and follow IHP orders. Elevated ketones typically require contacting the parent/guardian and possibly EMS.

Documentation — What Must Always Be Recorded
  • Date and time of the blood glucose check
  • Blood glucose reading
  • Ketone result (if tested)
  • Any insulin administered (dose, route, site, time)
  • Glucagon administered (dose, site, time)
  • Student symptoms and observations
  • Actions taken (snack given, parent called, 911 called)
  • Student's response to treatment
Sharps Disposal

All sharps (lancets, syringes, pen needles, infusion sets) must be disposed of in a hard plastic sharps container. The container is sealed and labeled before disposal. Follow your school district's policy for sharps disposal — this supersedes individual student preferences.

NeverPlace uncapped needles or lancets in regular trash. Always use a sharps container and follow district and universal precautions policy.

Insulin & Glucagon Administration

Safe administration, calculation, and emergency response

Types of Insulin Delivery
DeviceDescriptionUDCA Notes
Insulin syringeDraws from vial, injects subcutaneouslyUse correct syringe; verify dose with IHP
Insulin penPre-loaded cartridge with dial-doseAttach new needle; dial dose per IHP
Insulin pumpContinuous delivery via infusion setKnow how to bolus; know emergency disconnect; follow written pump instructions
Insulin Calculation — The Three-Step Method
Always Follow the IHPInsulin doses are NEVER calculated from memory. Every dose must come from the student's IHP and DMTP. The formulas below reflect how those plans are structured.
  • Step 1 — Carb Coverage: Total grams of carbs eaten ÷ Insulin-to-Carb Ratio = Coverage units
  • Step 2 — Correction Factor: (Current blood glucose − Target blood glucose) ÷ Correction Factor = Correction units
  • Step 3 — Total Dose: Coverage units + Correction units = Total units to administer

Glucagon Administration
Glucagon is an emergency medicationGlucagon raises blood glucose rapidly. It is used ONLY when a student is unconscious or cannot swallow safely.
  • Verify student is unconscious or unable to swallow
  • Prepare glucagon using the manufacturer's instructions for the specific product (kit, auto-injector, or nasal spray)
  • Calculate the correct dose per the student's IHP
  • Place student on their side (recovery position)
  • Administer glucagon per the IHP instructions
  • Call 911 immediately after administering
  • Then call the parent/guardian
  • Record time, dose, and site
  • When consciousness is regained, give carbohydrates per the DMTP
Subcutaneous Injection Technique
  • Wash hands; wear latex-free gloves
  • Prepare the correct dose using aseptic technique
  • Clean the injection site with an antiseptic wipe and allow to dry
  • Pinch skin gently; insert needle at the prescribed angle
  • Inject insulin slowly; withdraw needle; apply gentle pressure
  • Dispose of needle in sharps container immediately
  • Record time, dose, and injection site

Legal Protections, Nutrition & School Activities

Student rights, meal planning, field trips, and schedule disruptions

Federal and State Legal Protections
Section 504 — Rehabilitation Act

Prohibits discrimination against students with disabilities, including diabetes, in programs receiving federal funding. Most school diabetes accommodations are formalized through a Section 504 plan.

Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)

Extends non-discrimination protections for people with disabilities to all public entities, including public schools.

HSC Chapter 168 — Texas

State-specific requirements for UDCA training, IHP development, and student self-management rights. Students cannot be restricted from self-managing their diabetes anywhere on campus.

Nutrition for Students with Diabetes

Understanding nutrition is critical because food directly affects blood glucose. Key concepts for UDCAs:

  • Students with diabetes CAN eat foods containing sugar — carbohydrates must be counted and insulin adjusted accordingly
  • Regular meals are essential — skipping meals disrupts glucose control
  • Snacks are a clinical tool, not a treat — they are specifically ordered to prevent hypoglycemia
  • Physical activity may require additional snacks before or during exercise per the IHP
  • Illness, exam stress, growth spurts, and seasonal changes can all affect blood glucose unpredictably
Field Trips and Off-Campus Activities
HSC §168.006 — Transportation RequirementSchool districts must provide an information sheet to every employee responsible for transporting or supervising a student with diabetes during an off-campus activity. This sheet must: identify the student, list potential emergencies and appropriate responses, and provide emergency contact phone numbers.

For field trips and extracurricular events:

  • A trained UDCA (or school nurse) must accompany the student or be immediately reachable
  • The student must be permitted to carry all necessary diabetes supplies and equipment
  • The student's IHP applies at all school-sponsored events, on or off campus
  • Changes in schedule, timing of meals, and physical demands of the trip can affect blood glucose — monitor more closely
  • Emergency glucagon and glucose supplies must travel with the student
When Schedules Are Disrupted

Illness, exams, celebrations, and special events disrupt the normal school day. Any schedule change that affects meal timing, physical activity, or stress level can impact blood glucose. UDCAs should:

  • Anticipate disruptions and review the IHP for specific guidance on schedule changes
  • Monitor blood glucose more frequently on disrupted days
  • Communicate with the student and parents about upcoming schedule changes
  • Never allow a meal or snack to be skipped without following IHP guidance
  • Contact the school nurse or parent if blood glucose is persistently out of range during disruptions
Annual Training Update Requirements

Per TDC guidelines, UDCAs must complete annual training to assess knowledge and skills. Additionally, UDCAs must be informed in writing of any changes to a student's DMTP or IHP. At the beginning of each semester, UDCAs must review each student's current diabetes plan.
